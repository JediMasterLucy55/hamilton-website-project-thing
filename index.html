Part 1 - Hamilton and Lafeyette

The Battle of Yorktown, 1781. Monsieur Hamilton. Monsieur Lafayette. In command where you belong. How you say, no sweat? We’re finally on the field, we’ve had quite a run. Immigrants: We get the job done. So what happens if we win? I go back to France I bring freedom to my people if I’m given the chance. We’ll be with you when you do. Go, lead your men. I’ll see you on the other side. ‘Til we meet again, let’s go!

This song discusses the decisive victory of the now United State against in their war of independence against the British. The song starts off with Alexander Hamilton talking to his friend Marquis de Lafayette, who came to the US from France to fight in the Continental Army after a dinner with the Duke of Gloucester who told him about it. When Lafeyette says “in command where you belong”, he refers to Hamilton who, having longed for a command position in the army, finally got to lead a group in an assault during the battle. Lafeyette talks about how if the US gets independence, he will return to his home in France and start a revolution against the French monarchy, which he does help do. When Hamilton replies, “We’ll be with you when you do”, the irony is not lost as not much later, Hamilton and his party champion neutrality in any conflicts regarding Britain and France and therefore not helping in the French Revolution.

Part 2 - Hamilton is a Leader

I am not throwin' away my shot. I am not throwin' away my shot. Ay, yo, I'm just like my country. I'm young, scrappy and hungry. And I'm not throwin' away my shot. I am not throwin' away my shot. 'Til the world turns upside down. 'Til the world turns upside down. I imagine death so much it feels more like a memory. This is where it gets me. On my feet, the enemy ahead of me. If this is the end of me, at least I have a friend with me. Weapon in my hand, a command, and my men with me. Then I remember my Eliza's expecting me. Not only that, my Eliza's expecting. We gotta go, gotta get the job done. Gotta start a new nation, gotta meet my son! Take the bullets out your gun. What? The bullets out your gun. What? We move under cover and we move as one. Through the night, we have one shot to live another day. We cannot let a stray gunshot give us away. We will fight up close, seize the moment and stay in it. It's either that or meet the business end of the bayonet. The code word is "Rochambeau," dig me? Rochambeau! You have your orders now, go, man, go.

This part of the song starts with a callback to an earlier song in the musical, showing Hamilton’s growth to the position he has now. The song continues to describe Hamilton leading the assault on Redoubt 10, a British fortification. He again, repeats the line “I imagine death so much it feels more like a memory” from the beginning of the show, which he also says again at the end of the show; in a way it foreshadows his death by the hands of Aaron Burr during a duel who was technically “an enemy in front of [him]”. Hamilton then brings up his wife, Eliza, and how she is pregnant with their first kid, Phillip. It gives him a burst of fighting spirit as the song becomes more upbeat and Hamilton commands his soldiers to “take the bullets out your gun” as to not accidentally fire a shot and give them away in the assault. They used the name of Rochambeau, a French soldier who came to help the independence effort, as a codeword to signal when to attack.

Part 3 - Hamilton Talks About His Friends

And so the American experiment begins, With my friends all scattered to the winds. Laurens is in South Carolina, redefinin' bravery. We'll never be free until we end slavery. When we finally drive the British away. Lafayette is there, waiting in Chesapeake Bay How did we know that this plan would work? We had a spy on the inside, that's right Hercules Mulligan! A tailor spyin' on the British government. I take their measurements, information. And then I smuggle it. Up! To my brother's revolutionary covenant, I'm runnin' with the Sons of Liberty and I am lovin' it. See, that's what happens when you up against the ruffians. We in the sh*t now, somebody's gotta shovel it. Hercules Mulligan, I need no introduction. When you knock me down, I get the f*ck back up again! Let's go! Hahaha, woo! Left, right, hold! Go! What? What? What?

The “American experiment” being referred to is the idea of America itself, the democratic system, the first nation of its kind in the modern world. Hamilton proclaims that the experiment has begun as he tells the audience what the rest of his friends were doing during the battle. John Laurens, historically, had been working as a diplomat and also participated in the Assault of Redoubt 10. He was not “redefinin’ bravery” until after the battle ended, where he went to South Carolina in an attempt to form an all-Black military battalion. John Laurens was known for his abolitionist ideas against slavery, and did act according to his beliefs. Lafayette, on the contrary, was actually waiting in Chesapeake Bay and aided in the final defeat of the British and facilitating their surrender. Hercules Mulligan, an Irish immigrant, was an established tailor who catered to high-class British people. He would acquire sensitive information from his clients and deliver it as a member of the Committee of Correspondence. He was also a member of the Sons of Liberty.

Part 4 - They Won

After a week of fighting, a young man in a red coat stands on a parapet. We lower our guns as he frantically waves a white handkerchief. And just like that, it's over. We tend to our wounded, we count our dead. Black and white soldiers wonder alike if this really means freedom. Not yet. We negotiate the terms of surrender. I see George Washington smile. We escort their men out of Yorktown. They stagger home single file. Tens of thousands of people flood the streets. There are screams and church bells ringing. And as our fallen foes retreat, I hear the drinking song they're singing. The world turned upside down. The world turned upside down. The world turned upside down. The world turned upside down, down. Down, down, down. Freedom for America, freedom for France! Down, down, down. Gotta start a new nation, gotta meet my son! Down, down, down. We won! We won! We won! We won! The world turned upside down!

The Battle of Yorktown finally ended with General Cornwallis’s surrender, nearly securing American independence. Around 88 American soldiers and allied soldiers perished, with 301 wounded. According to Lin Manuel Miranda himself, Laurens’s line “Black and white soldiers wonder alike if this really means freedom” and Washington’s “Not yet” are referring to how the war technically wasn’t over: British troops still occupied the areas in the South. Laurens will die in a skirmish with these remaining troops. During the British retreat, there was no evidence that they actually sang “The World Turned Upside Down”. This is a popular tale though and can also be interpreted in another way. The British had the world’s biggest empire and most powerful military at the time, while their American colonies that barely had a military beat them in a war for their independence. In the sense of a rag-tag militia, or even a “volunteer army in need of a shower” (Hamilton reference!) defeating a powerful military force, it could seem like the world was mixed-up or upside down.
